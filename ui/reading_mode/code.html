4. Knowing What to Do

It is the duty of machines and those who design them to understand people. It is not our duty to understand the arbitrary, meaningless dictates of machines. The reasons for the deficiencies in human-machine interaction are numerous. Some come from the limitations of today’s technology. Some come from self-imposed restrictions by the designers, often to hold down cost. But most of the problems come from a complete lack of understanding of the design principles necessary for effective human-machine interaction.

Why this deficiency? Because much of the design is done by engineers who are experts in technology but limited in their understanding of people. “We are people ourselves,” they think, “so we understand people.” But in fact, we humans are amazingly complex. Those who have not studied human behavior often think it is pretty simple.

Engineers, moreover, make the mistake of thinking that logical explanation is sufficient: “If only people would read the instructions,” they say, “everything would be all right.”

"Good design is actually a lot harder to notice than poor design, in part because good designs fit our needs so well that the design is invisible."

When I was visiting a prominent engineering school, I asked my hosts if they knew how to operate the projector in the lecture hall. “Oh yes,” they replied, “we have it all figured out. We just push this button, wait exactly three seconds, and then push this one.” They were proud of their accomplishment.

I pointed out that the very fact they had to learn a special sequence of operations meant the design was flawed. “Why should you have to wait three seconds?” I asked. They looked at me in surprise. It had never occurred to them that the machine should be designed to accommodate them, rather than the other way around.
